Is it symmetric?

A laminate is a stack of thin fiber plies. Pull it, and the order of the plies decides if it stays flat, curls, twists, or shears.

Learn

1. Plies and fiber angles

Each ply has its fibers in one direction. The angle \(\theta\) goes from the x axis (the pull) to the fibers. A ply at −90° is the same as a ply at +90°, because a fiber has no front and back.

A laminate code lists the plies from the top to the bottom: [0/+45/90] has a 0° ply on top. A subscript s means "mirrored": [0/+45/90]s is [0/+45/90/90/+45/0].

2. The stiffness of one ply

In its own axes (1 along the fibers, 2 across them), a thin ply follows Hooke's law with the reduced stiffness matrix \([Q]\) (Kaw Eq. 2.93):

\[ [Q] = \begin{bmatrix} Q_{11} & Q_{12} & 0 \\ Q_{12} & Q_{22} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & Q_{66} \end{bmatrix} \]

\[ Q_{11} = \frac{E_1}{1 - \nu_{12}\nu_{21}}, \qquad Q_{22} = \frac{E_2}{1 - \nu_{12}\nu_{21}} \]

\[ Q_{12} = \frac{\nu_{12} E_2}{1 - \nu_{12}\nu_{21}}, \qquad Q_{66} = G_{12} \]

\[ \nu_{21} = \nu_{12}\,\frac{E_2}{E_1} \]

Graphite/epoxy (Kaw Table 2.1) has \(E_1 = 181\) GPa, \(E_2 = 10.3\) GPa, \(\nu_{12} = 0.28\), and \(G_{12} = 7.17\) GPa. Along the fibers, the ply is about 18 times stiffer than across them.

In the x-y axes of the laminate, a ply at the angle \(\theta\) has the transformed stiffness \([\bar{Q}]\) (Kaw Eq. 2.104, here in matrix form, with \(c = \cos\theta\) and \(s = \sin\theta\)):

\[ [\bar{Q}] = [T_\varepsilon]^{\mathsf{T}} \, [Q] \, [T_\varepsilon] \]

\[ [T_\varepsilon] = \begin{bmatrix} c^2 & s^2 & cs \\ s^2 & c^2 & -cs \\ -2cs & 2cs & c^2 - s^2 \end{bmatrix} \]

At 0° and 90°, \(\bar{Q}_{16} = \bar{Q}_{26} = 0\). At any other angle they are not zero: a pull along x also shears the ply.

3. From plies to a laminate

Each ply k lies between the heights \(h_{k-1}\) (top) and \(h_k\) (bottom), measured from the mid-plane (Kaw Eq. 4.20). The plies add up to three matrices (Kaw Eq. 4.28):

\[ \begin{aligned} A_{ij} &= \sum_k (\bar{Q}_{ij})_k \,(h_k - h_{k-1}) \\ B_{ij} &= \frac{1}{2} \sum_k (\bar{Q}_{ij})_k \,(h_k^2 - h_{k-1}^2) \\ D_{ij} &= \frac{1}{3} \sum_k (\bar{Q}_{ij})_k \,(h_k^3 - h_{k-1}^3) \end{aligned} \]

They connect the forces \(N\) and the moments \(M\) per unit width with the mid-plane strains \(\varepsilon^0\) and the curvatures \(\kappa\) (Kaw Eq. 4.29):

\[ \begin{bmatrix} N \\ M \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} A & B \\ B & D \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \varepsilon^0 \\ \kappa \end{bmatrix} \]

\([A]\) is the stretch stiffness, \([D]\) is the bend stiffness, and \([B]\) couples them. In this lesson the load is only a pull, \(N_x = 100\) N/mm, with no moment. The model solves the 6 equations for \(\varepsilon^0\) and \(\kappa\).

4. Symmetry and [B]

A laminate is symmetric if the plies above the mid-plane are the mirror image of the plies below it: the same material, angle, and thickness (Kaw Section 5.2.1).

In \([B]\), the term \((h_k^2 - h_{k-1}^2)\) of a ply above the mid-plane has the opposite sign of the term of its mirror ply below it. In a symmetric stack the terms cancel, so \([B] = 0\). Then a pull gives no curvature: the plate stays flat.

If the stack is not symmetric, \([B] \neq 0\). A pull then also bends or twists the plate. In the stack view, the brackets on the right compare each ply with its mirror ply: solid if they match, dashed if they do not.

5. Balance and A16, A26

A laminate is balanced if each ply at +θ has a ply at −θ of the same material and thickness. The two plies do not need to be next to each other. 0° and 90° plies need no partner (Kaw Section 5.2.5).

\(\bar{Q}_{16}\) and \(\bar{Q}_{26}\) change sign with the angle: \(\bar{Q}_{16}(-\theta) = -\bar{Q}_{16}(\theta)\). In \([A]\) the height term \((h_k - h_{k-1})\) is only the ply thickness, so the position does not matter, and each ±θ pair cancels. A balanced laminate has \(A_{16} = A_{26} = 0\): a pull does not shear it.

Balance and symmetry are different. [+45/−45] is balanced but not symmetric: it twists. [+45/−45]s is both: it stays flat.

6. What a pull does

The plate does not show its small stretch. It shows the bend and the shear, much larger than they are, so you can see them. A darker color means that a point moves more.

The heart of the calculation, the ABD matrix:

def abd_matrix(angles: list[float], thickness: float) -> np.ndarray:
    """Return the 6x6 ABD matrix. Ply 1 is the top ply (z = -h/2)."""
    n_plies = len(angles)
    z = thickness * (np.arange(n_plies + 1) - n_plies / 2.0)
    q = reduced_stiffness(MATERIAL)
    a = np.zeros((3, 3))
    b = np.zeros((3, 3))
    d = np.zeros((3, 3))
    for k in range(n_plies):
        te = strain_transform(angles[k])
        qbar = te.T @ q @ te
        a = a + qbar * (z[k + 1] - z[k])
        b = b + qbar * (z[k + 1] ** 2 - z[k] ** 2) / 2.0
        d = d + qbar * (z[k + 1] ** 3 - z[k] ** 3) / 3.0
    return np.block([[a, b], [b, d]])
